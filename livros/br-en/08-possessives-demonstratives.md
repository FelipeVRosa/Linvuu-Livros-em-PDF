# Possessives, demonstratives, liking and preferring

By the end of this lesson you can say whose something is without confusing "his", "her" and "your", point at things near and far the way Brazilians do, say what you like, love and prefer, and use {{muito}} and {{pouco}} without making them agree when they should not.

## 8.1 A scene you will live

You share an apartment in Belo Horizonte with two Brazilians. On Saturday morning your phone is at 3% and you cannot find your charger. There are three chargers on the living-room table, and you have to ask about each of them.

> **Emma:** {{Bruno, esse carregador é seu?}}
> **Bruno:** {{Não, é da Clara. O meu está no quarto. Aquele ali, o preto, é o meu.}}
> **Emma:** {{E esses óculos?}}
> **Bruno:** {{São dele. Do Rafael. Ele deixa tudo aqui na sala.}}
> **Emma:** {{E essa mochila azul? É sua?}}
> **Bruno:** {{É minha, sim. Você gosta dela?}}
> **Emma:** {{Gosto muito. Mas eu prefiro a verde.}}

Translation: *Bruno, is this charger yours? / No, it is Clara's. Mine is in the bedroom. That one over there, the black one, is mine. / And these glasses? / They are his. Rafael's. He leaves everything here in the living room. / And this blue backpack? Is it yours? / It is mine, yes. Do you like it? / I like it a lot. But I prefer the green one.*

Six small problems are hiding in that conversation: {{seu}} (your) next to {{dele}} (his), {{esse}} next to {{aquele}}, {{aqui}} next to {{ali}}, the little word {{de}} that follows {{gostar}} ({{gosta dela}}), the verb {{preferir}}, and {{muito}} used as an adverb. This lesson takes them one at a time.

Words you will need, with their guides:

| Portuguese | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| {{o carregador}} | `oo kah-heh-gah-DOR` | the charger |
| {{os óculos}} | `ooss AW-koo-looss` | the glasses (always plural) |
| {{a mochila}} | `ah moh-SHEE-lah` | the backpack |
| {{o guarda-chuva}} | `oo GWAR-dah SHOO-vah` | the umbrella |
| {{a sala}} | `ah SAH-lah` | the living room |
| {{o quarto}} | `oo KWAR-too` | the bedroom |
| {{deixar}} | `day-SHAR` | to leave (something somewhere); to let |

## 8.2 The trap: English counts owners, Portuguese counts things

English has one small word for each owner: *my, your, his, her, our, their*. The word never changes with the thing owned: *my brother, my sister, my brothers, my sisters*. If the owner is a woman, you say *her brother*, and the "her" tells you about the owner.

Portuguese works the other way round. The possessive agrees with the **thing owned**, in gender and number, exactly like an adjective. It says nothing about the owner's sex.

| English | Portuguese | What agrees |
|---|---|---|
| my brother | {{meu irmão}} | masculine singular thing |
| my sister | {{minha irmã}} | feminine singular thing |
| my brothers | {{meus irmãos}} | masculine plural thing |
| my sisters | {{minhas irmãs}} | feminine plural thing |
| **her** brother | {{o irmão dela}} | (see 8.4: the owner is named separately) |
| **his** brother | {{o irmão dele}} | the same |

So a woman says {{meu irmão}} and a man says {{minha irmã}}. Both are "my". The word follows the noun, not the speaker. This is the first of two traps. The second is that "his", "her" and "your" are tangled together in the third person, and that is where {{dele}} and {{dela}} come in.

## 8.3 The possessives of the speaker, of the listener and of "us"

Learn the full table once. The forms for *I* and *we* are easy to use because nothing is ambiguous about them.

| Owner | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Masc. pl. | Fem. pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| my (eu) | {{meu}} `MEH-oo` | {{minha}} `MEE-nyah` | {{meus}} `MEH-ooss` | {{minhas}} `MEE-nyahss` |
| your (você) | {{seu}} `SEH-oo` | {{sua}} `SOO-ah` | {{seus}} `SEH-ooss` | {{suas}} `SOO-ahss` |
| our (nós, a gente) | {{nosso}} `NAW-soo` | {{nossa}} `NAW-sah` | {{nossos}} `NAW-sooss` | {{nossas}} `NAW-sahss` |
| your (tu, in some regions) | {{teu}} `TEH-oo` | {{tua}} `TOO-ah` | {{teus}} `TEH-ooss` | {{tuas}} `TOO-ahss` |

Remember that **{{você}} takes {{seu}}** because it is grammatically a third-person word, as you learned with the pronouns: {{você tem}} uses the same verb form as {{ele tem}}. In the south of Brazil and in parts of the north and northeast, people say {{tu}} and use {{teu}} and {{tua}}. You should recognize them; you can always use {{você}} and {{seu}} and be understood everywhere.

::: passo 1 Choose the form by the noun
Find the noun, decide its gender and number, and match it. The speaker's sex does not matter.
:::

{{meu pai}}, {{minha mãe}}, {{meu carro}}, {{minha casa}}, {{meus amigos}}, {{minhas amigas}}.

::: passo 2 Decide whether to add the article
In Brazil you may say {{meu pai}} or {{o meu pai}}, {{minha casa}} or {{a minha casa}}. Both are correct and both are common. The article gives a slightly more careful sound, and in Portugal it is used more often than in Brazil. After the verb {{ser}} used to say whose something is, you normally drop it: {{O carro é meu}} (not "é o meu", unless you are choosing between several cars).

Next to a name, the article stays optional in the same way: {{o meu amigo Bruno}}, {{meu amigo Bruno}}.
:::

::: passo 3 Use the possessive alone as a pronoun
You do not need a second word for "mine", "yours", "ours" as English does. The same word works, with the article when it replaces a noun:

- {{Esse carregador é seu?}} (Is this charger yours?)
- {{Não, o meu está no quarto.}} (No, mine is in the bedroom.)
- {{Os nossos vizinhos são simpáticos. E os seus?}} (Our neighbors are nice. And yours?)
:::

::: passo 4 Say "a friend of mine" with the possessive after the noun
English moves to *of mine*. Portuguese does it with the possessive after an indefinite noun and **no article**:

- {{um amigo meu}} (a friend of mine)
- {{uma prima minha}} (a cousin of mine)
- {{uns colegas nossos}} (some colleagues of ours)
:::

::: dica
When the owner is obvious, Portuguese often drops the possessive and says only *the*. This is common with body parts and with things that belong to the subject: {{Eu lavo as mãos}} (I wash my hands), {{Ela perdeu a carteira}} (she lost her wallet; the owner is clear from the subject). English insists on *my* and *her* here.
:::

## 8.4 The big ambiguity: seu, sua, dele, dela

Look at this sentence:

> {{Ana viu Paulo com a sua irmã.}}

It can mean *Ana saw Paulo with Ana's sister*, *with Paulo's sister* or *with your sister* (if you were talking to Ana, Paulo or someone else). {{Seu}} and {{sua}} can mean *your*, *his*, *her* and *their*. Portuguese speakers know this and avoid it.

The fix is simple, and Brazilians use it all the time: for "his", "her" and "their", leave {{seu}} aside and say **de + the owner**. With a pronoun, {{de}} and {{ele}} fuse into one word:

| Owner | Say | Example |
|---|---|---|
| his | {{dele}} `DEH-lee` | {{o carro dele}} (his car) |
| her | {{dela}} `DEH-lah` | {{a casa dela}} (her house) |
| their (masc. or mixed) | {{deles}} `DEH-leess` | {{os filhos deles}} (their children) |
| their (all women) | {{delas}} `DEH-lahss` | {{as amigas delas}} (their friends) |
| a named owner | {{do Paulo}}, {{da Ana}} | {{o carro do Paulo}}, {{a irmã da Ana}} |
| the person you address | {{seu}}, {{sua}}, {{de você}} | {{o seu carro}} |

Notice what changes. The word before {{dele}} or {{dela}} agrees with the **thing owned** ({{o carro}}, {{a casa}}), and {{dele/dela}} agrees with the **owner**. The result is exactly the information English gives you with *his car* and *her car*.

So the speaker of Brazilian Portuguese says:

| Message | Brazilian way |
|---|---|
| Ana saw Paulo with Paulo's sister. | {{A Ana viu o Paulo com a irmã dele.}} |
| Ana saw Paulo with her own sister. | {{A Ana viu o Paulo com a irmã dela.}} |
| Ana saw Paulo with your sister. | {{A Ana viu o Paulo com a sua irmã.}} (to the listener) |

::: atencao
In spoken Brazilian Portuguese, {{seu}} and {{sua}} almost always mean **your** (you = {{você}}). When someone means "his" or "her", they say {{dele}} or {{dela}}. In writing, and in some older speakers' speech, {{seu}} can still mean "his" or "her", so you will read it. In conversation you can use the rule: **{{seu}} is yours; {{dele/dela/deles/delas}} is theirs.**
:::

For "our", use {{nosso}} or the colloquial {{da gente}}, which is natural with {{a gente}} as the subject: {{Esse carro é da gente}} = {{Esse carro é nosso}}. {{O apartamento da gente é pequeno}}.

### Whose is it?

To ask whose something is, use {{de quem}}. The answer uses {{ser}} + the possessive, or {{de}} + the owner:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| {{De quem é esse guarda-chuva?}} | {{É meu.}} / {{É do Rafael.}} / {{É dela.}} |
| {{De quem são esses óculos?}} | {{São do Bruno.}} / {{São dele.}} |

{{De}} fuses with the article of the owner: {{de + o}} = {{do}}, {{de + a}} = {{da}}, {{de + os}} = {{dos}}, {{de + as}} = {{das}}. This is the same contraction you used in the lesson on articles. With a name used without article ({{de Clara}}), nothing fuses, but many Brazilians put an article before a first name in conversation ({{a Clara}}, {{o Rafael}}), so you will hear {{da Clara}} and {{do Rafael}} all the time. Both are acceptable.

## 8.5 This, that, that over there: the demonstratives

English has two steps: *this* (near) and *that* (far). Portuguese is traditionally taught with three, tied to the three persons:

| Near whom? | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Masc. pl. | Fem. pl. | Neutral |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| me (the speaker) | {{este}} `EHS-tchee` | {{esta}} `EHS-tah` | {{estes}} `EHS-tcheess` | {{estas}} `EHS-tahss` | {{isto}} `EES-too` |
| you (the listener) | {{esse}} `EH-see` | {{essa}} `EH-sah` | {{esses}} `EH-seess` | {{essas}} `EH-sahss` | {{isso}} `EE-soo` |
| neither of us, far | {{aquele}} `ah-KEH-lee` | {{aquela}} `ah-KEH-lah` | {{aqueles}} `ah-KEH-leess` | {{aquelas}} `ah-KEH-lahss` | {{aquilo}} `ah-KEE-loo` |

That is the textbook. Here is what Brazilians do with it in daily speech.

::: passo 1 Use esse, essa, esses, essas for both "this" and "that" near you
In everyday speech, {{este}}, {{esta}} and {{isto}} are heard far less often than {{esse}}, {{essa}} and {{isso}}. A Brazilian holding a cup will often say {{Gosto desse café}} meaning *I like this coffee*, even though the textbook says {{deste}}. You will still read {{este/esta/isto}} in newspapers, signs and formal talks, and you will hear them in careful speech, so you must recognize them. For your own speech, {{esse/essa/isso}} is the safe default.
:::

::: passo 2 Use aquele, aquela, aqueles, aquelas for things far from both of you
That includes things far in distance (the building at the end of the street), far in time (**{{naquela época}}**, *in those days*), or something you are not sure how to name.
:::

::: passo 3 Use the neutral forms when you do not name the thing
{{Isto}}, {{isso}} and {{aquilo}} never agree: they refer to something you cannot or do not want to name, or to a whole idea.

- {{O que é isso?}} (What is this / that?)
- {{Aquilo ali é um banco?}} (Is that over there a bank?)
- {{Isso mesmo!}} (Exactly!)
- {{Não gosto disso.}} (I do not like this / that.)
:::

### Essa × aquela in a real choice

Hold two shirts, one in each hand, in a shop in Recife. In the textbook, your hand has {{esta camisa}} and the display behind the cashier has {{aquela camisa}}. In speech, you say {{essa camisa}} for the one in your hand, and {{aquela}} for the one behind the counter. If the clerk points to a shirt next to you and asks {{Quer experimentar essa?}}, the {{essa}} points to something close to you both.

::: comparacao
| In English | In Portuguese |
|---|---|
| *this / that* (two steps) | {{esse}} (close) and {{aquele}} (far): two steps in speech, three in the textbook |
| *this* and *that* are used alone as pronouns: *I like this* | alone as a pronoun for an unnamed thing: {{isso}} / {{aquilo}}; for a named thing, {{esse}} + noun or the noun is dropped: {{Gosto desse}} |
| *that* can refer to what was just said: *That's true* | {{Isso é verdade}}; {{Esse problema é grande}} refers to a problem already mentioned |
:::

### The contractions

Demonstratives fuse with {{em}} and {{de}}, as articles do. Say them aloud, because they are among the most frequent words in the language.

| Preposition | + esse | + essa | + isso | + aquele | + aquela | + aquilo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| {{de}} | {{desse}} `DEH-see` | {{dessa}} `DEH-sah` | {{disso}} `DJEE-soo` | {{daquele}} `dah-KEH-lee` | {{daquela}} `dah-KEH-lah` | {{daquilo}} `dah-KEE-loo` |
| {{em}} | {{nesse}} `NEH-see` | {{nessa}} `NEH-sah` | {{nisso}} `NEE-soo` | {{naquele}} `nah-KEH-lee` | {{naquela}} `nah-KEH-lah` | {{naquilo}} `nah-KEE-loo` |

The same happens with {{este/esta/isto}}: {{deste}}, {{desta}}, {{disto}}, {{neste}}, {{nesta}}, {{nisto}}. You will read them more than you say them.

- {{Eu moro nesse prédio.}} (I live in this building.)
- {{Você gosta dessa música?}} (Do you like this song?)
- {{Não tenho nada a ver com aquilo.}} (I have nothing to do with that.)

With {{a}} (to), the feminine forms of {{aquele}} fuse too and take a grave accent: {{Vou àquela festa}}. This is the mark you saw in Lesson 1 under "acento grave", and you will meet it in reading before you use it in speech.

## 8.6 Aqui, aí, ali, lá: the place words that go with them

The demonstratives have a matching set of place words.

| Word | Guide | Where | Goes with |
|---|---|---|---|
| {{aqui}} | `ah-KEE` | here, near me | {{este}} / {{esse}} |
| {{aí}} | `ah-EE` | there, near you or just there | {{esse}} |
| {{ali}} | `ah-LEE` | over there, within sight | {{aquele}} |
| {{lá}} | `lah` | far away, often out of sight | {{aquele}} |
| {{cá}} | `kah` | here (mostly with movement: {{vem cá}}) | |

- {{Venha cá!}} / {{Vem aqui!}} (Come here!)
- {{Tem pão aí?}} (Is there bread there? Said by phone to someone at home.)
- {{O banheiro fica ali, à direita.}} (The bathroom is over there, on the right.)
- {{Meus pais moram lá em Recife.}} (My parents live there in Recife.)

::: dica
**The Brazilian habit:** pair the demonstrative with the place word. Do it every time you point. It sounds natural and makes your meaning clearer than the demonstrative alone.

- {{Quero esse aqui.}} (I want this one.)
- {{Você pode pegar essa aí pra mim?}} (Can you get that one for me?)
- {{Aquele ali é o meu ônibus.}} (That one over there is my bus.)
:::

::: passo 1 Combine them with the right verb
Use {{estar}} for location, as you learned: {{Meu celular está aqui}}. Use {{ficar}} for a fixed location of a building, a street or a city: {{O banco fica ali}}. Both appear in the same conversation.
:::

## 8.7 Gostar: you always need "de"

In English you *like* something. The verb takes a direct object. In Portuguese, {{gostar}} always needs the preposition {{de}} before the thing or person that you like. The verb is a regular {{-ar}} verb, like the ones in the lesson on the present tense.

| Person | Form | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| eu | {{gosto}} | `GOHS-too` |
| você, ele, ela, a gente | {{gosta}} | `GOHS-tah` |
| nós | {{gostamos}} | `gohs-TAH-mooss` |
| vocês, eles, elas | {{gostam}} | `GOHS-tah~oo` |

Then add {{de}} and the thing. If a noun with an article follows, {{de}} fuses with the article as in 8.4:

| You like… | Say |
|---|---|
| a noun without article | {{Eu gosto de café.}} |
| a noun with article | {{Eu gosto do Rafael.}} / {{Ela gosta da casa.}} / {{Gostamos dos vizinhos.}} |
| a person, as a pronoun | {{Eu gosto dele.}} / {{Eu gosto dela.}} / {{Ela gosta de mim.}} / {{Gosto de você.}} |
| a verb | {{Eu gosto de cozinhar.}} (infinitive, no change) |
| a demonstrative | {{Gosto disso.}} / {{Gosto desse.}} |

Notice {{gosto dele}}, not "gosto de ele": the two words fuse. After a preposition, "me" becomes {{mim}}: {{Ela gosta de mim}} (not "de eu"). We will return to this in Lesson 12.

::: atencao
**Only an infinitive follows {{gostar de}}.** Not a conjugated verb, and not "-ing": {{Gosto de dançar}} (I like dancing), never "gosto dançar". The small word {{de}} is easy to forget when you are focusing on the verb. Keep it.
:::

### How much do you like it?

Put the intensity next to the verb, never before it:

| Portuguese | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| {{Gosto muito de samba.}} | `GOHS-too MOO~ee-too djee SAH~-bah` | I like samba a lot. |
| {{Gosto bastante.}} | `GOHS-too bahs-TAH~-tchee` | I like it quite a lot. |
| {{Gosto um pouco.}} | `GOHS-too oo~ POH-koo` | I like it a little. |
| {{Não gosto muito.}} | `NOW~ GOHS-too MOO~ee-too` | I do not like it much. |
| {{Não gosto nada.}} | `NOW~ GOHS-too NAH-dah` | I do not like it at all. |
| {{Adoro viajar.}} | `ah-DAW-roo vee-ah-ZHAR` | I love traveling. |
| {{Detesto esperar.}} | `deh-TEHS-too ehs-peh-RAR` | I hate waiting. |
| {{Odeio fila.}} | `oh-DAY-oo FEE-lah` | I hate lines. |

Notice that {{adorar}}, {{detestar}} and {{odiar}} take **no** {{de}}: {{adoro café}}, {{detesto trânsito}}. They are the exceptions that confirm the rule. Only {{gostar}} needs the preposition.

### Gostar × amar

Use {{gostar}} for almost everything: food, music, places, people you like. Use {{amar}} `ah-MAR` for strong, deep love, mostly of people: {{Eu amo meus filhos}}, {{Eu te amo}}. In casual speech people also say {{Eu amo pizza}} or {{Eu amo esse lugar}}, a slightly enthusiastic way to say *I love it*, but {{adorar}} is the more neutral word for things you are very fond of.

Be careful with people. {{Eu gosto de você}} is gentle: it means *I like you* and can be a friendly compliment or, depending on the tone, a first step toward romance. {{Eu te amo}} is much stronger and is not said to a friend lightly.

## 8.8 Preferir: choosing between two things

{{Preferir}} `preh-feh-REER` is an {{-ir}} verb whose stem keeps the same vowel. Its present tense is regular enough to learn at a glance:

| Person | Form | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| eu | {{prefiro}} | `preh-FEE-roo` |
| você, ele, ela, a gente | {{prefere}} | `preh-FEH-ree` |
| nós | {{preferimos}} | `preh-feh-REE-mooss` |
| vocês, eles, elas | {{preferem}} | `preh-FEH-reh~ee` |

It takes a direct object, so there is no extra preposition before the thing preferred:

- {{Eu prefiro a mochila verde.}} (I prefer the green backpack.)
- {{Prefiro café.}} (I prefer coffee.)
- {{Ela prefere ficar em casa.}} (She prefers to stay at home.)
- {{Nós preferimos viajar de ônibus.}} (We prefer to travel by bus.)

To say what you prefer **to** what, the traditional preposition is {{a}}:

- {{Prefiro café a chá.}} (I prefer coffee to tea.)
- {{Prefiro ficar em casa a sair.}} (I prefer staying in to going out.)

In everyday Brazilian speech you will also hear {{Prefiro café do que chá}} and {{Prefiro café que chá}}. Teachers and style guides may correct them. If you want a safe, natural alternative, there is one that nobody corrects: {{Gosto mais de café do que de chá}} (I like coffee more than tea). The comparison with {{mais … do que}} is explained in Lesson 13.

::: erros
| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Eu prefiro mais café. | {{Eu prefiro café.}} | {{preferir}} already means "like more"; do not add {{mais}} |
| Eu gosto café. | {{Eu gosto de café.}} | {{gostar}} always takes {{de}} |
| Eu gosto de ela. | {{Eu gosto dela.}} | {{de}} + {{ela}} fuse into one word |
| Ela gosta de eu. | {{Ela gosta de mim.}} | after a preposition, "me" is {{mim}} |
| Eu adoro de pizza. | {{Eu adoro pizza.}} | {{adorar}}, {{amar}}, {{odiar}} take no {{de}} |
:::

## 8.9 Muito and pouco: sometimes they agree and sometimes they do not

{{Muito}} (a lot, much, many, very) and {{pouco}} (little, few, not much) are among the most frequent words in the language. They have two jobs, and the grammar is different for each. Misusing them is a recognizable beginner mark.

**Job 1: adjective before a noun.** The word is a quantity (*a lot of*, *little*). It agrees in gender and number with the noun, like any adjective.

| Meaning | Masc. sing. | Fem. sing. | Masc. pl. | Fem. pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a lot, many | {{muito}} | {{muita}} | {{muitos}} | {{muitas}} |
| little, few | {{pouco}} | {{pouca}} | {{poucos}} | {{poucas}} |

- {{Tem muito trânsito hoje.}} (There is a lot of traffic today.)
- {{Tem muita gente na praia.}} (There are lots of people at the beach.) Notice that {{gente}} is singular, so the verb is singular.
- {{Tenho muitos amigos aqui.}} (I have many friends here.)
- {{Ela tem poucas amigas.}} (She has few female friends.)
- {{Temos pouco tempo.}} (We have little time.)

**Job 2: adverb.** When the word modifies a verb, an adjective or another adverb, it never agrees. It stays {{muito}} or {{pouco}}.

- {{Eu gosto muito de samba.}} (I like samba a lot.) Verb.
- {{Elas são muito simpáticas.}} (They are very nice.) Adjective: not "muitas simpáticas".
- {{Ele fala pouco.}} (He does not talk much.)
- {{Você fala muito bem.}} (You speak very well.) Adverb.

Test: can you replace the word with *very* (or *a lot*, if there is no noun)? Then it is an adverb and does not change. If you can say *a lot of* / *a little of* the noun, it is a quantity word and it agrees.

::: atencao
**{{Muito obrigado}} or {{muito obrigada}}?** In both cases {{muito}} stays the same, because it modifies {{obrigado}} as an adverb. The ending of {{obrigado/a}} follows the speaker, as you learned in Lesson 1.
:::

### Pouco and um pouco are opposites

This is one of the traps that costs foreigners the most. The article changes the meaning completely.

| Portuguese | Meaning | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| {{Falo pouco português.}} | I speak little Portuguese. | negative: not enough |
| {{Falo um pouco de português.}} | I speak a little Portuguese. | positive: some |
| {{Tenho pouco dinheiro.}} | I have little money. | not enough |
| {{Tenho um pouco de dinheiro.}} | I have some money. | enough to do something |

When you want to be modest and polite, say {{Falo um pouco de português}}. If you say {{falo pouco}}, a Brazilian may hear a complaint.

Three more words of quantity, and when to use them:

| Word | Guide | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| {{bastante}} | `bahs-TAH~-tchee` | quite a lot, enough | {{Eu como bastante.}} |
| {{demais}} | `djee-MIESS` | too much; also "a lot, really" | {{Gosto demais!}} (I really love it!) |
| {{nada}} | `NAH-dah` | not at all, nothing | {{Não gosto nada.}} |

{{Demais}} has two lives. In a negative or critical sentence it means "too much": {{Tem gente demais aqui}} (there are too many people here). In an enthusiastic sentence it means "really, so much": {{Esse bolo está bom demais}} (this cake is so good).

## 8.10 Putting it all together

Read this text aloud, then answer the two questions after it.

> {{Eu moro com dois amigos em Belo Horizonte. O apartamento é pequeno, mas a sala é grande e tem muita luz. No meu quarto tem uma cama, uma mesa e a minha mochila azul. Os óculos do Rafael estão sempre na sala, o carregador da Clara está sempre na cozinha e o meu celular nunca está comigo! Eu gosto muito dos meus amigos, mas eu prefiro quando a casa está calma. Aquele sofá ali é velho, mas é o meu lugar preferido. Eu adoro ler nele.}}

Translation: *I live with two friends in Belo Horizonte. The apartment is small, but the living room is big and has a lot of light. In my bedroom there is a bed, a table and my blue backpack. Rafael's glasses are always in the living room, Clara's charger is always in the kitchen and my phone is never with me! I like my friends a lot, but I prefer it when the house is calm. That sofa over there is old, but it is my favorite place. I love reading on it.*

1. Why is it {{os óculos do Rafael}} and not "os óculos dele" in the second sentence? (Both are possible; one uses the name, one the pronoun.)
2. What does {{muita luz}} agree with?

New words in the text: {{a cama}} `ah KAH-mah` (bed), {{a cozinha}} `ah koh-ZEE-nyah` (kitchen), {{o sofá}} `oo soh-FAH` (sofa), {{calma}} `KAH-oo-mah` (calm), {{sempre}} `SEH~-pree` (always), {{nunca}} `NOO~-kah` (never).

## 8.11 The mistakes English speakers make

::: erros
| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ele ama sua mãe. (meaning: his mother) | {{Ele ama a mãe dele.}} | {{sua}} is ambiguous; in speech {{sua}} means "your" |
| O dela carro é novo. | {{O carro dela é novo.}} | {{dele/dela}} go after the noun; the article agrees with the thing owned |
| Ela é minha irmão. | {{Ele é meu irmão.}} | the possessive agrees with the noun ({{irmão}} is masculine) |
| Eu gosto música. | {{Eu gosto de música.}} | {{gostar}} always takes {{de}} |
| Eu gosto de dançando. | {{Eu gosto de dançar.}} | an infinitive follows {{gostar de}}, never a "-ing" form |
| Muitas pessoas são muitas simpáticas. | {{Muitas pessoas são muito simpáticas.}} | {{muito}} as an adverb does not agree |
| Falo pouco de português, por favor. | {{Falo um pouco de português.}} | {{pouco}} means "not enough", {{um pouco}} means "some" |
| Eu quero esse aí aqui. | {{Quero esse aqui.}} | pick one place word to match the demonstrative |
| O que é isto aí? | {{O que é isso?}} | {{isso}} is the natural spoken word |
:::

## 8.14 Pocket summary: Lesson 8

::: resumo
- **Possessives agree with the thing owned**, not the owner: {{meu}}, {{minha}}, {{meus}}, {{minhas}}; {{seu}} (your), {{nosso}}. The article is optional: {{meu pai}} / {{o meu pai}}.
- **He, she, they:** {{dele}}, {{dela}}, {{deles}}, {{delas}} (after the noun). In speech, {{seu}} means "your".
- **Whose?** {{De quem é?}} — {{É meu}} / {{É do Rafael}} / {{É dela}}. A friend of mine: {{um amigo meu}}.
- **Demonstratives:** {{esse/essa}} (close; the spoken default), {{aquele/aquela}} (far), {{isso/aquilo}} (neutral). {{Este/esta/isto}} are formal or written.
- **Place words:** {{aqui}} (here), {{aí}} (there with you), {{ali}} (over there), {{lá}} (far). Pair them: {{esse aqui}}, {{essa aí}}, {{aquele ali}}.
- **Contractions:** {{desse}}, {{nesse}}, {{disso}}, {{nisso}}, {{daquele}}, {{naquele}}, {{daquilo}}, {{naquilo}}.
- **Gostar de** + noun or infinitive: {{gosto de café}}, {{gosto de cozinhar}}, {{gosto dela}}, {{gosta de mim}}. {{Adorar}}, {{amar}}, {{odiar}}, {{detestar}} take no {{de}}.
- **Preferir** takes no {{mais}}: {{prefiro café a chá}}; safe alternative: {{gosto mais de café do que de chá}}.
- **Muito/pouco:** before a noun they agree ({{muita gente}}, {{poucos amigos}}); before a verb or adjective they do not ({{muito simpáticas}}). {{Pouco}} = not enough; {{um pouco}} = some.
:::
