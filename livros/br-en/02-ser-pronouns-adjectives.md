# Ser, pronouns and adjectives: who you are

By the end of this lesson you can say who you are, where you are from, what you do and how old you are; introduce someone else; describe people with adjectives that agree in gender and number; and understand why Portuguese so often leaves out the word for "I".

## 2.1 A scene you will live

It is the first evening of a Portuguese course in Rio de Janeiro. Emma, a teacher from Canada, is holding a plastic cup of juice and trying not to look lost. Camila, a classmate, walks up to her.

::: dialogo
**Camila:** {{Oi! Eu sou a Camila. E você?}}
**Emma:** {{Oi, Camila! Eu sou a Emma. Sou do Canadá.}}
**Camila:** {{Que legal! Eu sou de Recife, mas agora moro aqui. Esse é o Pedro. Ele é argentino.}}
**Pedro:** {{Muito prazer! Sou engenheiro. E você, o que faz?}}
**Emma:** {{Sou professora. Vocês são amigos?}}
**Camila:** {{A gente é colega de trabalho. Nós somos muito diferentes, mas a gente se dá bem.}}
:::

*Camila: Hi! I'm Camila. And you? / Emma: Hi, Camila! I'm Emma. I'm from Canada. / Camila: How cool! I'm from Recife, but now I live here. This is Pedro. He's Argentinian. / Pedro: Nice to meet you! I'm an engineer. And you, what do you do? / Emma: I'm a teacher. Are you two friends? / Camila: We're colleagues. We're very different, but we get along well.*

Read the scene again and notice three things. First, nobody says {{eu sou engenheiro}}: Pedro says only {{sou engenheiro}}. Second, nobody says "a" or "an" before the profession: {{sou professora}}, not "I am a teacher" word for word. Third, the word for "we" appears in two forms, {{nós}} and {{a gente}}, and Camila uses both in a single breath. (Note also {{Eu sou a Camila}}: Brazilians often put {{o}} or {{a}} before a first name in speech. Lesson 3 returns to this.) That is how real Brazilians talk, and this lesson explains all three.

Some words in the scene are ready-made blocks for now. {{Moro}} (I live) and {{faz}} (do/does) come back in Lesson 4, and {{esse}} (this, near you) in Lesson 8. Learn them as phrases and move on.

| Word | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| {{legal}} | `leh-GAH-oo` | cool, nice, great |
| {{Que legal!}} | `kee leh-GAH-oo` | How cool! |
| {{muito prazer}} | `MOO~ee-too prah-ZEHR` | nice to meet you |
| {{diferentes}} | `djee-feh-REH~-tcheess` | different (plural) |
| {{colega}} | `koh-LEH-gah` | colleague, classmate |
| {{agora}} | `ah-GAW-rah` | now |

## 2.2 The trap: three habits to leave at the door

English builds every sentence from the same bricks: a subject, a verb, and a form of "to be" that barely changes. Three of those habits cause most of the early mistakes in Portuguese.

1. **"I am" needs the pronoun.** In English you cannot say "am a teacher". In Portuguese the verb ending already tells you who is speaking, so the pronoun is often missing.
2. **"You" is one word.** English has a single *you*. Portuguese has {{você}} for one person and {{vocês}} for several, and the verb changes with them.
3. **Adjectives are not frozen.** *Tall* is *tall* for a man, a woman or ten people. Portuguese adjectives take the gender and number of the person described: {{alto}}, {{alta}}, {{altos}}, {{altas}}.

There is a fourth trap, and you will meet it in 2.11: English says "I am twenty", Portuguese says "I have twenty years".

## 2.3 The subject pronouns

A **subject pronoun** is the word that says who does the verb: *I, you, he, she, we, they*. Here is the full set you need for Brazilian Portuguese.

| Meaning | Portuguese | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| I | {{eu}} | `EH-oo` |
| you (one person) | {{você}} | `voh-SEH` |
| he | {{ele}} | `EH-lee` |
| she | {{ela}} | `EH-lah` |
| we | {{nós}} | `NAWSS` |
| we (spoken) | {{a gente}} | `ah ZHEH~-tchee` |
| you (several people) | {{vocês}} | `voh-SEHSS` |
| they (men, or mixed) | {{eles}} | `EH-leess` |
| they (women only) | {{elas}} | `EH-lahss` |

::: passo 1 Use "você" for one person and "vocês" for more than one
{{Você}} is the everyday word for *you* in most of Brazil, with friends, colleagues, strangers and bosses. It works at any age and in almost any situation. Its plural is {{vocês}}, and there is no formal-informal split in the plural.
:::

::: passo 2 Remember that "você" takes the same verb as "ele/ela"
Grammatically, {{você}} counts as a third person. The verb you use with {{você}} is the same form you use with {{ele}} and {{ela}}. That is the reason Portuguese verb tables look so short: only a few forms are really different. You will see this at once in the next section.
:::

::: passo 3 Choose "nós" or "a gente" by register
{{Nós}} is correct everywhere and is typical of writing, speeches and careful speech. {{A gente}} (literally "the people") is what most Brazilians say when they talk to each other: {{A gente vai?}} (Shall we go?). It takes the same verb as {{ele/ela}}, so it is easy: {{a gente é}}. You can safely use both; learn to understand both from the first day.
:::

::: passo 4 Say "eles" for a mixed group
If even one man is in the group, Portuguese says {{eles}}. {{Elas}} is only for groups made up entirely of women. The same rule works for adjectives and nouns: one man and ten women together are still {{eles}}, {{amigos}}, {{brasileiros}}.
:::

### Tu, o senhor and a senhora

You will hear three more words.

- **{{Tu}}** `too` also means *you* (one person). It is used in the South of Brazil, in much of the North and Northeast, and by some speakers in Rio. Depending on region and speaker, it takes either the {{ele}} form of the verb ({{tu é}}, {{tu fala}}) or its own ending ({{tu és}}, {{tu falas}}). In São Paulo, Minas Gerais and most of the Southeast, people say {{você}} instead. You will not need to say {{tu}}; you will need to understand it.
- **{{O senhor}}** `oo seh-NYOR` and **{{a senhora}}** `ah seh-NYOH-rah` are polite forms for an older person or someone you want to treat with respect, such as a customer, a doctor or a grandparent. They take the {{ele/ela}} verb too.
- **{{Vós}}** is the old plural of *you*. You will find it in the Bible and in classical texts. Nobody says it today.

::: dica
**Always safe:** {{você}} for one person, {{vocês}} for many. If someone calls you {{tu}} and you answer with {{você}}, nobody will notice.
:::

## 2.4 The verb ser

**Ser** `SEHR` is one of the two Portuguese verbs for "to be". (The other, {{estar}}, arrives in Lesson 5. For now, do not worry about it.) {{Ser}} tells you **who** or **what** someone or something is: identity, origin, profession, nationality, and permanent or defining qualities. Its present tense is irregular, which means you must learn it as it is. Learn it first.

| Person | Form | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| eu | {{sou}} | `SOH` | I am |
| você / ele / ela / a gente | {{é}} | `eh` | you are / he is / she is / we are |
| nós | {{somos}} | `SOH-mooss` | we are |
| vocês / eles / elas | {{são}} | `SOW~` | you are / they are |

Notice what the table shows. There are only **four** forms, and the second one, {{é}}, serves for four different subjects. Learn {{sou}}, {{é}}, {{somos}}, {{são}} and you can build hundreds of sentences.

The form {{é}} is also the word *is*, as in {{O Brasil é grande}} (Brazil is big). And {{são}} is also *are*: {{As aulas são boas}}.

::: comparacao
| English | Portuguese |
|---|---|
| I am, you are, he is, we are, they are: five forms | {{sou}}, {{é}}, {{é}}, {{somos}}, {{são}}: four different forms, one for several subjects |
| "You are" is the same for one person and for many | {{você é}} (one person) ≠ {{vocês são}} (several) |
| "It is" needs a subject (*it*) | {{É difícil}} = It is difficult. No subject word at all. |
:::

The pronunciation of {{sou}} gives many English speakers trouble. The **ou** is a plain *oh*, as you saw in Lesson 1: {{sou}} `SOH`, not "sow".

## 2.5 The subject can disappear

In English, the subject is compulsory: *am a student* is not a sentence. In Portuguese, the verb ending already tells you who is acting, so the pronoun is optional. Compare:

| With the pronoun | Without the pronoun |
|---|---|
| {{Eu sou estudante.}} | {{Sou estudante.}} |
| {{Nós somos amigos.}} | {{Somos amigos.}} |
| {{Eles são brasileiros.}} | {{São brasileiros.}} |

In everyday conversation, speakers drop the pronoun when the context makes the person clear, and keep it when they want emphasis or contrast: {{Eu sou de Recife, e você?}}

There is one important limit. With {{é}}, the verb alone could mean you, he, she or we-with-a-gente, so Brazilians usually keep the pronoun or the name:

- {{É professor.}} Who? You cannot tell.
- {{Ele é professor.}} / {{Você é professor?}} / {{O Pedro é professor.}} Now it is clear.

::: atencao
Dropping the subject is a feature of **all** verbs, but it is much safer to drop **eu** and **nós** than **ele/ela/você**. When {{é}} appears alone, listeners will not know who you mean. As a beginner, use the pronoun with {{é}} and drop it where you feel comfortable: {{Sou}}, {{Somos}}, {{São}}.
:::

### And what about "it"?

Portuguese has no word for *it* as a subject. English "It is cold" or "It is late" becomes {{Está frio}} and {{É tarde}}, with no subject at all. When you want to talk about an object, you use {{ele}} or {{ela}} (the object's grammatical gender decides): {{Gosto do carro. Ele é novo.}} Many learners are surprised to hear Brazilians say {{ele}} and {{ela}} for things, but it is normal.

## 2.6 Saying who you are: name, origin, profession

With {{ser}} you can already say most of what you need in a first meeting. Here are the patterns, with the exact questions Brazilians ask.

| You want to say | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| your name | {{Meu nome é…}} | {{Meu nome é Daniel.}} |
| your name (another way) | {{Eu me chamo…}} | {{Eu me chamo Daniel.}} |
| your name (short) | {{Eu sou o/a…}} | {{Eu sou o Daniel.}} |
| where you are from | {{Sou de + city}} | {{Sou de Manchester.}} |
| where you are from (country) | {{Sou do / da + country}} | {{Sou do Canadá. Sou da Irlanda.}} |
| your profession | {{Sou + profession}} | {{Sou designer.}} |
| your nationality | {{Sou + nationality}} | {{Sou irlandês.}} |

Here are the matching questions. Memorize them as blocks:

| Question | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| {{Como você se chama?}} | `KOH-moo voh-SEH see SHAH-mah` | What is your name? |
| {{Qual é o seu nome?}} | `KWAH-oo eh oo SEH-oo NOH-mee` | What is your name? |
| {{De onde você é?}} | `djee OH~-djee voh-SEH eh` | Where are you from? |
| {{Você é de onde?}} | `voh-SEH eh djee OH~-djee` | Where are you from? (spoken order) |
| {{O que você faz?}} | `oo kee voh-SEH fahss` | What do you do (for work)? |
| {{Quem é ele?}} | `KEH~ee eh EH-lee` | Who is he? |

::: passo 1 Use "de" with a city and "do/da" with a country
Cities take plain {{de}}: {{Sou de São Paulo}}, {{Sou de Londres}}. Most country names take an article, and {{de}} fuses with it: {{de + o = do}}, {{de + a = da}}. So {{Sou do Brasil}}, {{Sou do Canadá}}, {{Sou da Alemanha}}, {{Sou da França}}. Portugal and a few others take no article: {{Sou de Portugal}}. Lesson 3 explains these fusions, called contractions. For now learn the countries in the table in 2.10 together with their {{do/da}}.
:::

::: passo 2 Say your profession with no article
In English you say "I am **a** teacher". In Portuguese you say {{Sou professor}} (or {{professora}}), with no {{um/uma}}. The profession works like an adjective: it says what category you belong to. The same goes for nationality and religion: {{Ele é católico}}, {{Elas são brasileiras}}. You add {{um/uma}} only when you describe the person in a more detailed way: {{Ele é um professor muito bom}} (He is a very good teacher).
:::

::: passo 3 Match the ending to the person
Professions and nationalities have a masculine and a feminine form: {{professor / professora}}, {{médico / médica}}, {{brasileiro / brasileira}}. A man says {{Sou advogado}}; a woman says {{Sou advogada}}. The pattern is the same as {{obrigado / obrigada}} from Lesson 1.
:::

### Professions

| {{professor}} | {{professora}} | `proh-feh-SOR` / `proh-feh-SOH-rah` | teacher |
| {{médico}} | {{médica}} | `MEH-djee-koo` / `MEH-djee-kah` | doctor |
| {{engenheiro}} | {{engenheira}} | `eh~-zheh-NYAY-roo` / `eh~-zheh-NYAY-rah` | engineer |
| {{advogado}} | {{advogada}} | `ah-djee-voh-GAH-doo` / `ah-djee-voh-GAH-dah` | lawyer |
| {{enfermeiro}} | {{enfermeira}} | `eh~-fehr-MAY-roo` / `eh~-fehr-MAY-rah` | nurse |
| {{cozinheiro}} | {{cozinheira}} | `koh-zee-NYAY-roo` / `koh-zee-NYAY-rah` | cook |
| {{programador}} | {{programadora}} | `proh-grah-mah-DOR` / `proh-grah-mah-DOH-rah` | programmer |
| {{vendedor}} | {{vendedora}} | `veh~-deh-DOR` / `veh~-deh-DOH-rah` | salesperson |

| {{garçom}} | {{garçonete}} | `gar-SOH~` / `gar-soh-NEH-tchee` | waiter / waitress |

Some professions have **one form for both**: {{estudante}} `ehs-too-DAH~-tchee`, {{dentista}} `deh~-TCHEES-tah`, {{jornalista}} `zhor-nah-LEES-tah`, {{motorista}} `moh-toh-REES-tah` (driver), {{artista}} `ar-TCHEES-tah`. In those cases only the article shows the gender: {{o dentista}} (a man), {{a dentista}} (a woman). Words in **-or** usually add **-a** for the feminine ({{professor}}, {{professora}}), with the well-known exception {{ator}} / {{atriz}} (actor / actress).

::: dica
Your profession is not on the list? Ask a Brazilian: {{Como se diz "accountant" em português?}} (How do you say "accountant" in Portuguese?). The answer is {{contador}} or {{contadora}}.
:::

## 2.7 Adjectives: they agree with the person

An **adjective** describes a noun: *tall*, *nice*, *Brazilian*. In Portuguese, the adjective must **agree** with the noun: it takes the same gender (masculine or feminine) and the same number (singular or plural). In English this does not exist, so it feels strange at first, but the rules are few and very regular.

::: passo 1 Look at the ending
Most adjectives belong to one of three groups.

| Group | Masculine | Feminine | Guide (masc. / fem.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ends in **-o** | {{alto}}, {{bonito}}, {{simpático}} | change **-o** to **-a**: {{alta}}, {{bonita}}, {{simpática}} | `AH-oo-too` / `AH-oo-tah` |
| ends in **-e**, **-l**, **-z**, **-ista** | {{inteligente}}, {{legal}}, {{feliz}} | the **same** form for both: {{inteligente}}, {{legal}}, {{feliz}} | `ee~-teh-lee-ZHEH~-tchee`, `leh-GAH-oo`, `feh-LEES` |
| ends in **-or**, **-ês**, and nationalities in **-ão** | {{trabalhador}}, {{inglês}}, {{alemão}} | add **-a**, drop the accent: {{trabalhadora}}, {{inglesa}}, {{alemã}} | `trah-bah-lyah-DOR` / `trah-bah-lyah-DOH-rah` |
:::

Look at the last row. Adjectives in **-ês** lose the circumflex when they become feminine ({{francês}} → {{francesa}}) because the accent is no longer needed. The nationality {{alemão}} becomes {{alemã}}.

There are some adjectives that behave in slightly different ways. {{Bom}} becomes {{boa}} (`BOH-ah`) and {{mau}} becomes {{má}}, but you will normally say {{ruim}} (`hoo-EE~`, bad) which is the same for both genders. Most adjectives in **-l** do not change, but nationalities in **-ol** add **-a**: {{espanhol}} becomes {{espanhola}}.

::: passo 2 Add the number
Number is much simpler. For the plural, you add **-s** to a word that ends in a vowel; you add **-es** to words that end in **-r**, **-z**; and **-l** changes to **-is**. Lesson 3 gives the complete plural rules. One detail worth knowing now: an adjective in **-il** with the stress on the first syllable, like {{fácil}}, changes to **-eis**. For the adjectives you need now:

| Singular | Plural | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| {{alto}} / {{alta}} | {{altos}} / {{altas}} | `AH-oo-tooss` / `AH-oo-tahss` |
| {{inteligente}} | {{inteligentes}} | `ee~-teh-lee-ZHEH~-tcheess` |
| {{legal}} | {{legais}} | `leh-GIESS` |
| {{fácil}} (easy) | {{fáceis}} | `FAH-see-oo` / `FAH-sayss` |
| {{feliz}} | {{felizes}} | `feh-LEE-zeess` |
| {{trabalhador}} | {{trabalhadores}} | `trah-bah-lyah-DOH-reess` |
| {{alemão}} | {{alemães}} | `ah-leh-MAH~eess` |
| {{inglês}} | {{ingleses}} | `ee~-GLEH-zeess` |
:::

::: passo 3 Check the whole group
When an adjective describes several nouns, it goes in the plural, and one masculine noun is enough to make it masculine: {{O Pedro e a Camila são simpáticos}}. A mixed group is masculine, just like {{eles}}.
:::

A few high-frequency adjectives, with their opposites:

| Adjective | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|

| {{legal}} | `leh-GAH-oo` | nice, cool |
| {{inteligente}} | `ee~-teh-lee-ZHEH~-tchee` | smart |
| {{divertido / divertida}} | `djee-ver-TCHEE-doo` | fun |
| {{tímido / tímida}} | `TCHEE-mee-doo` | shy |
| {{alto / alta}} | `AH-oo-too` | tall |
| {{baixo / baixa}} | `BIE-shoo` | short |
| {{novo / nova}} | `NOH-voo` | new, young |
| {{velho / velha}} | `VEH-lyoo` | old |
| {{bonito / bonita}} | `boh-NEE-too` | pretty, nice-looking |
| {{grande}} | `GRAH~-djee` | big |
| {{pequeno / pequena}} | `peh-KEH-noo` | small |
| {{jovem}} | `ZHOH-veh~ee` | young |

{{Jovem}} (plural {{jovens}}) has one form for both genders. {{Novo}} usually means *new* for objects and *young* in a general way; for people you can say {{jovem}}.

### Intensifiers and position

The usual intensifiers are {{muito}} `MOO~ee-too` (very) and {{bem}} `BEH~ee` (quite, really), and they do not change: {{Ela é muito simpática}}, {{Eles são muito simpáticos}}. Also useful are {{um pouco}} `oo~ POH-koo` (a little) and {{bastante}} `bahs-TAH~-tchee` (quite).

The adjective normally goes **after** the noun: {{um amigo simpático}}, {{uma cidade grande}}. A few very common adjectives can go before the noun with a change in meaning: {{um grande amigo}} (a great friend) versus {{um amigo grande}} (a physically big friend). In this lesson, put the adjective after the noun and you will be correct.

## 2.8 Nationalities and countries

Nationalities are adjectives, so they agree. They are written with a **small letter** in Portuguese: {{brasileiro}}, {{americano}}. (Languages and nationalities are not capitalized, unlike English.) Countries are capitalized.

| {{a Austrália}} | `ow-STRAH-lee-ah` | {{australiano / australiana}} | `ow-strah-lee-AH-noo` |
| {{a Alemanha}} | `ah-leh-MAH-nyah` | {{alemão / alemã}} | `ah-leh-MOW~` |

When you do not know whether a country takes an article, check it once and memorize it with the name: {{o Brasil}}, {{a França}}. And when you want a plain, simple answer, use the nationality instead: {{Sou francesa}}, which needs no article.

::: atencao
As in English, one word names both the language and the people, but Portuguese changes the article: {{o inglês}} is the English language ({{Falo inglês}}, I speak English), and {{um inglês}} is an Englishman. Languages are never capitalized.
:::

## 2.9 Describing people with ser

You can now put the pieces together. With {{ser}} you describe what someone is like, in a lasting way: character, appearance, origin, profession. Say these sentences aloud.

| Portuguese | Meaning |
|---|---|
| {{O Pedro é argentino e é muito divertido.}} | Pedro is Argentinian and very funny. |
| {{A Camila é de Recife e é muito simpática.}} | Camila is from Recife and is very friendly. |
| {{Eu sou tímida, mas a Emma é muito divertida.}} | I'm shy, but Emma is very fun. |
| {{Nós somos professores e eles são engenheiros.}} | We're teachers and they're engineers. |
| {{Vocês são jovens e inteligentes.}} | You're young and smart. |
| {{O inglês é difícil, mas o português é bonito.}} | English is difficult, but Portuguese is beautiful. |

The last sentence shows something you will study in Lesson 3: when a language is the **subject** of the sentence, Portuguese puts the article {{o}} before it ({{O português é bonito}}). After {{falar}}, the article usually disappears: {{Falo português}}.

Now compare {{ser}} questions and answers, which follow the same words:

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| {{Você é brasileiro?}} | {{Sou, sim.}} / {{Não, não sou.}} |
| {{A Camila é professora?}} | {{É, sim.}} / {{Não, não é.}} |
| {{Vocês são casados?}} | {{Somos, sim.}} / {{Não, não somos.}} |

In Portuguese a yes-no question has the same word order as a statement: you only raise the pitch of your voice at the end. Lesson 4 explains this. To answer "yes" you repeat the verb ({{Sou}}), and often add {{sim}} for warmth. To answer "no", you say {{não}} twice: first as the answer, then in front of the verb.

## 2.10 Age: ser is the wrong verb

English says *I am twenty-five*. Portuguese says **I have twenty-five years**. The verb is {{ter}}, not {{ser}}.

| English | Portuguese | Guide |
|---|---|---|
| I am thirty. | {{Tenho trinta anos.}} | `TEH-nyoo TREE~-tah AH-nooss` |
| How old are you? | {{Quantos anos você tem?}} | `KWAH~-tooss AH-nooss voh-SEH TEH~ee` |
| She is twenty-five. | {{Ela tem vinte e cinco anos.}} | `EH-lah TEH~ee VEE~-tchee ee SEE~-koo AH-nooss` |

You will learn {{ter}} properly in Lessons 4 and 5, and the numbers in Lesson 6. For now, keep the block {{Tenho ___ anos}} and the question {{Quantos anos você tem?}} Do not forget the word {{anos}}: it is the normal way to say your age.

## 2.11 Read a real text

Read the text aloud, first with the guide, then without it.

> {{Oi, eu sou a Carla. Sou brasileira, de Porto Alegre. Tenho vinte e oito anos e sou arquiteta. O Marco é meu marido. Ele é italiano e é muito simpático.}}

Guide: `oy, EH-oo SOH ah KAR-lah. SOH brah-zee-LAY-rah, djee POR-too ah-LEH-gree. TEH-nyoo VEE~-tchee ee OY-too AH-nooss ee SOH ar-kee-TEH-tah. oo MAR-koo eh MEH-oo mah-REE-doo. EH-lee eh ee-tah-lee-AH-noo ee eh MOO~ee-too see~-PAH-tchee-koo.`

Translation: *Hi, I'm Carla. I'm Brazilian, from Porto Alegre. I'm twenty-eight and I'm an architect. Marco is my husband. He's Italian and very friendly.*

Notice what you can do already. You know that {{sou}} has no {{eu}} in front of it, that the profession ({{arquiteta}}) takes no article, that the age uses {{tenho}}, and that every adjective agrees: Carla is {{brasileira}} and Marco is {{italiano}}. The word {{meu}} (my) belongs to Lesson 8, but you can already use {{meu marido}}, {{meu nome}}.

## 2.12 The mistakes English speakers make

::: erros
| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| {{Eu é professor.}} | {{Eu sou professor.}} | {{é}} is for {{você, ele, ela, a gente}}; with {{eu}} you need {{sou}} |
| {{Eles é brasileiros.}} | {{Eles são brasileiros.}} | the plural of {{é}} is {{são}}: {{eles}} and {{vocês}} take {{são}} |
| {{Nós é amigos.}} | {{Nós somos amigos.}} | {{nós}} takes {{somos}} (while {{a gente}} takes {{é}}) |
| {{Sou um professor.}} | {{Sou professor.}} | professions and nationalities take no article |
| {{Eu sou 25 anos.}} | {{Eu tenho 25 anos.}} | in Portuguese you *have* years; you are not years |
| {{Ela é alto.}} | {{Ela é alta.}} | the adjective agrees with *ela*: feminine |
| {{Eles são brasileiro.}} | {{Eles são brasileiros.}} | plural subject, plural adjective |
| {{Ela é um brasileira.}} | {{Ela é brasileira.}} | no article, and the adjective already shows gender |
| {{Eu sou de o Brasil.}} | {{Eu sou do Brasil.}} | {{de + o}} always becomes {{do}} |
| {{Eu sou Brasileira.}} | {{Eu sou brasileira.}} | nationalities start with a small letter |
| {{Sou de São Paulo e é muito grande.}} | {{Sou de São Paulo. A cidade é muito grande.}} | with {{é}} alone, nobody knows the subject; name it |
| {{Eu sou ele.}} ("It's me") | {{Sou eu.}} | to say "it is I", the verb comes first and agrees with {{eu}}: {{sou eu}}, {{é ele}}, {{somos nós}} |
:::

## 2.15 Pocket summary: Lesson 2

::: resumo
- **Pronouns:** {{eu, você, ele, ela, nós / a gente, vocês, eles, elas}}. {{Você}} and {{a gente}} take the {{ele/ela}} verb. {{Tu}} exists (South, North, Northeast); {{o senhor / a senhora}} is polite.
- **Ser:** {{sou}}, {{é}}, {{somos}}, {{são}}. It names identity, origin, nationality, profession and lasting qualities.
- **The subject can disappear:** {{Sou estudante.}} Keep the pronoun with {{é}} when it would otherwise be unclear.
- **No article** before profession or nationality: {{Sou professora}}, not *um/uma professora*.
- **Origin:** {{Sou de + city}}; {{Sou do / da + country}} ({{de + o = do}}, {{de + a = da}}).
- **Adjectives agree:** -o → -a; -e, -l, -z, -ista stay; -or → -ora; -ês → -esa; {{alemão}} → {{alemã}}. Plural: -s; -l → -is; -r, -z → -es; {{alemão}} → {{alemães}}.
- **A mixed group is masculine:** {{eles}}, {{brasileiros}}.
- **Age:** {{Tenho trinta anos}}, never *sou trinta*.
- **Questions to remember:** {{Como você se chama?}}, {{De onde você é?}}, {{O que você faz?}}, {{Quantos anos você tem?}}
:::
