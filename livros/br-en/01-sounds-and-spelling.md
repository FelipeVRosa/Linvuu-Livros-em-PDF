# Sounds and spelling: how to read Portuguese out loud

By the end of this lesson you can read any Portuguese word aloud and be understood, spell your name with the Portuguese letter names, put the stress on the right syllable, and say the polite phrases you will use every day in Brazil.

## 1.1 A scene you will live

You walk into a padaria in São Paulo at 7 a.m. The woman behind the counter says, very fast: {{Bom dia! Tudo bem? O que vai ser?}} You understand almost nothing, and you panic. Then she points at the coffee machine, and you hear yourself say {{Um café, por favor}}. She smiles. {{Pra viagem?}} You nod. You have just had your first conversation in Portuguese.

Everything in this lesson exists to make that moment calm instead of frightening. Not grammar yet, not vocabulary yet: **sound**. If your mouth knows the sounds, every word you learn afterwards arrives already pronounceable. If it does not, every word is a small struggle.

## 1.2 The good news: you already know more than you think

Look at these words. You can already read most of them, and Brazilians use them every day:

| English | Portuguese | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| chocolate | {{chocolate}} | `sho-ko-LAH-tchee` |
| hotel | {{hotel}} | `oh-TEH-oo` |
| banana | {{banana}} | `bah-NAH-nah` |
| family | {{família}} | `fah-MEE-lyah` |
| important | {{importante}} | `eem-por-TAH~-tchee` |
| restaurant | {{restaurante}} | `heh-stow-RAH~-tchee` |
| animal | {{animal}} | `ah-nee-MAH-oo` |
| nation | {{nação}} | `nah-SOW~` |

Thousands of English words come from Latin, and so does Portuguese, so you start with a huge vocabulary. The words look familiar; the **sounds** are different. That is exactly what this lesson fixes.

The second piece of good news is that Portuguese spelling is far more regular than English. English has "though", "through", "tough" and "cough". Portuguese has rules, and they nearly always work. Learn the rules once and you can read new words with confidence.

::: dica
**The only secret of pronunciation:** listen first, then repeat out loud. Reading silently teaches your eyes, not your mouth. Every example in this book has audio in the Linvuu course at linvuu.com.br. Use it.
:::

## 1.3 How to read the pronunciation guides in this book

Next to many Portuguese words you will see a guide like `bah-NAH-nah`. It is an approximation written with English sounds, so you can read it without learning a new alphabet. The rules are few:

| In the guide | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| CAPITAL LETTERS | the stressed syllable (say it stronger and longer) | {{obrigado}} `oh-bree-GAH-doo` |
| `~` after a vowel | nasal vowel: air goes through the nose (Lesson 1.6) | {{não}} `NOW~` |
| `tch` and `dj` | as in *church* and *judge* | {{noite}} `NOY-tchee`, {{dia}} `DJEE-ah` |
| `zh` | as the *s* in *measure* | {{hoje}} `OH-zhee` |
| `sh` | as in *ship* | {{xícara}} `SHEE-kah-rah` |
| `ly`, `ny` | the Portuguese *lh* and *nh* | {{filho}} `FEE-lyoo`, {{amanhã}} `ah-mah-NYAH~` |
| `h` | the Brazilian *r* at the start of a word: like English *h* in *hot* | {{rua}} `HOO-ah` |
| `oo` at the end | the Portuguese final *l* or *-o*: like *oo* in *zoo* | {{Brasil}} `brah-ZEE-oo` |

The guide is a bridge, not the destination. After a few weeks you will stop needing it. Its limits are honest ones: it cannot show the difference between the open and the closed *e* or *o* (the accent marks do that, see 1.5), and it cannot show the melody of a sentence. For those, listen.

## 1.4 The alphabet and the letter names

Portuguese uses the same 26 letters as English. The letters **k**, **w** and **y** are used mostly in foreign words and abbreviations (the 2009 spelling reform made them official). What changes is the **name** of each letter, and you need the names whenever someone asks you to spell something: your name at a hotel, your e-mail at a bank, your street for a delivery.

::: atencao
Four names trip up English speakers every time. **G** is not *gee*: it is {{gê}}. **J** is not *jay*: it is {{jota}}. **E** is not *ee*: it is {{é}}. **I** is not *eye*: it is {{i}}, like the *ee* in *see*. Say them out loud ten times; people will hear them constantly.
:::

**The letter H is silent.** {{Hotel}} `oh-TEH-oo`, {{hoje}} `OH-zhee`, {{homem}} `OH~-meh~ee`. It only does something inside two digraphs: {{ch}} `sh` and {{lh}} `ly`, and {{nh}} `ny` (see 1.8).

To spell an e-mail, say {{arroba}} for @ and {{ponto}} for the dot: {{ana ponto silva arroba exemplo ponto com ponto bê erre}} is ana.silva@exemplo.com.br.

## 1.5 The oral vowels

Portuguese writes five vowel letters (a, e, i, o, u), but you hear more than five sounds, because **e** and **o** each have an open and a closed version. The vowels are **pure**: unlike English, you do not glide. English speakers say *day* as "day-ee" and *go* as "go-oo". In Portuguese, stop the sound before the glide starts.

::: passo 1 Learn the five clean vowels
Keep your tongue and lips still while the sound lasts. Do not let it slide.
:::

| Letter | Sound | Like English… | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| a (stressed) | open *ah* | *father* | {{casa}} `KAH-zah`, {{pá}} `pah` |
| e (stressed, é) | open *eh* | *bed* | {{café}} `kah-FEH`, {{pé}} `peh` |
| e (stressed, ê) | closed *eh* | *say*, but cut off before the glide | {{você}} `voh-SEH`, {{mesa}} `MEH-zah` |
| i | *ee* | *see* | {{aqui}} `ah-KEE`, {{vida}} `VEE-dah` |
| o (stressed, ó) | open *aw* | *thought* (US: *dog*) | {{avó}} `ah-VAW`, {{pó}} `paw` |
| o (stressed, ô) | closed *oh* | *go*, but cut off before the glide | {{avô}} `ah-VOH`, {{povo}} `POH-voo` |
| u | *oo* | *food* | {{uva}} `OO-vah`, {{tudo}} `TOO-doo` |

::: passo 2 Know that the accent marks tell you open or closed
The acute accent (**´**) marks a stressed vowel that is **open**: {{café}}, {{avó}}, {{pé}}. The circumflex (**^**) marks a stressed vowel that is **closed**: {{você}}, {{avô}}, {{bebê}}. Without a mark, you will learn the sound word by word. The difference matters because it can change the meaning: {{avó}} is *grandmother* and {{avô}} is *grandfather*.
:::

::: passo 3 Weaken the vowels at the end of a word
When a vowel is **unstressed and at the end of a word**, it becomes weak and short:

- final **-e** sounds like a short *ee*: {{noite}} `NOY-tchee`, {{grande}} `GRAH~-djee`, {{desculpe}} `djees-KOOL-pee`
- final **-o** sounds like a short *oo*: {{tudo}} `TOO-doo`, {{obrigado}} `oh-bree-GAH-doo`, {{livro}} `LEE-vroo`
- final **-a** is a relaxed *ah*, close to the last vowel of *sofa*: {{casa}}, {{pessoa}} `peh-SOH-ah`
:::

::: erros
| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| oh-bree-GAH-doh | `oh-bree-GAH-doo` | in {{obrigado}} the final -o is a weak *oo* |
| NOY-teh | `NOY-tchee` | in {{noite}} the final -e is a weak *ee*, and *t* before *i* becomes *tch* |
| voh-SAY-ee | `voh-SEH` | in {{você}} there is no glide: stop the vowel cleanly |
| kah-FAY | `kah-FEH` | in {{café}}, é is an open *eh*, as in *bed* |
:::

## 1.6 Nasal vowels: the sound English does not have

This is the single biggest surprise for English speakers, and mastering it changes how your whole Portuguese sounds. A **nasal vowel** is a vowel pronounced with air flowing through the nose as well as the mouth. You make it by saying the vowel while thinking of the *ng* of *sing*, **without** closing your mouth on a final *n* or *m*.

English has nasal vowels only as a side effect (the vowel in *sing* or *can't* is slightly nasal), never as a sound of its own. Portuguese uses them to tell words apart, and they are among the most frequent sounds in the language.

::: passo 1 Spot the nasal signs
Portuguese marks nasal vowels in three ways:

- a **tilde** (**~**) over **ã** and **õ**: {{mãe}}, {{pão}}, {{limões}}
- a vowel followed by **m** or **n** before another consonant: {{tempo}}, {{onde}}, {{campo}}, {{quinto}}, {{um}}
- a final **-m** after a vowel: {{bom}}, {{sim}}, {{bem}}, {{tem}}, {{um}}
:::

::: passo 2 Say the vowel through the nose, and stop
The *m* or *n* is not a separate consonant sound. It is only the sign that the vowel is nasal. Do not close your lips on {{bom}}: say `BOH~` and stop.
:::

| Word | Guide | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| {{sim}} | `SEE~` | yes |
| {{bem}} | `BEH~ee` | well, fine |
| {{bom}} | `BOH~` | good |
| {{um}} | `OO~` | one, a |
| {{não}} | `NOW~` | no |
| {{pão}} | `POW~` | bread |
| {{mão}} | `MOW~` | hand |
| {{mãe}} | `MAH~ee` | mother |
| {{muito}} | `MOO~ee-too` | very, a lot |
| {{limão}} | `lee-MOW~` | lemon, lime |
| {{coração}} | `koh-rah-SOW~` | heart |
| {{pensar}} | `peh~-SAR` | to think |

Three endings need special attention because you will use them all the time:

- **-ão** (stressed): {{pão}}, {{não}}, {{coração}}. A nasal *ow*, one syllable.
- **-am** and **-em** (unstressed endings of third-person plural verbs): {{falam}} `FAH-lah~oo` ends in a short nasal *ow*; {{comem}} `KOH-meh~ee` ends in a nasal *ay*.
- **-ões** and **-ães**: the plural of words in {{-ão}}: {{limões}} `lee-MOH~eess`, {{pães}} `PAH~eess`.

::: comparacao
| In English | In Portuguese |
|---|---|
| "song", "sing": the *ng* is a consonant you close with the tongue | {{bom}}, {{sim}}: no consonant at all, only a nasal vowel |
| nasal sound is accidental and changes no meaning | {{pão}} (bread) ≠ {{pau}} (stick); {{mão}} (hand) ≠ {{mau}} (bad); {{lã}} (wool) ≠ {{lá}} (there) |
:::

::: erros
| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| NOWN | `NOW~` | in {{não}}, do not add a clear *n* at the end |
| BOHM, with closed lips | `BOH~` | in {{bom}}, close with the nose, not with the lips |
| PAH-oh (two syllables) | `POW~` | {{pão}} is a single syllable |
| SIM, as in *simple* | `SEE~` | in {{sim}} the vowel is *ee*, nasal |
:::

## 1.7 Diphthongs: two vowels in one syllable

A **diphthong** is two vowels pronounced in a single syllable, the second one weak. In Portuguese they are written with **i** or **u** as the second element.

| Written | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ai | *eye* | {{pai}} `pie` (father), {{mais}} `mice` (more) |
| ei | *ay* (as in *day*) | {{feito}} `FAY-too` (done), {{beijo}} `BAY-zhoo` (kiss) |
| oi | *oy* | {{oi}} `oy` (hi), {{noite}} `NOY-tchee` |
| ui | *oo-ee* | {{muito}} `MOO~ee-too`, {{fui}} `FOO-ee` |
| au | *ow* (as in *cow*) | {{causa}} `KOW-zah` (cause), {{pausa}} `POW-zah` |
| eu | *eh-oo* | {{meu}} `MEH-oo` (my), {{europeu}} `eh-oo-roh-PEH-oo` |
| ou | usually a short *oh* | {{ou}} `oh` (or), {{pouco}} `POH-koo` (little) |
| iu | *ee-oo* | {{viu}} `VEE-oo` (saw) |

::: dica
{{Ou}} is written with **u**, but in everyday Brazilian speech it is nearly a plain *oh*. Do not say *ow*: {{pouco}} is `POH-koo`, never "POW-koo".
:::

## 1.8 The consonants

Good news first: **b, d, f, k, m, n, p, t, v, z** sound much like English. Aim for a softer *t* and *d*, with the tongue touching the back of the upper teeth rather than the ridge behind them; do not puff air after *p*, *t* and *k*. Now the ones that need your attention.

### Seven consonants that work differently

| Letter(s) | Where | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **c** | before a, o, u | hard *k* | {{casa}} `KAH-zah`, {{cor}} `kor`, {{cuidado}} `kwee-DAH-doo` |
| **c** | before e, i | *s* | {{cedo}} `SEH-doo` (early), {{cinco}} `SEE~-koo` (five) |
| **ç** | before a, o, u | *s* | {{coração}}, {{açúcar}} `ah-SOO-kar` (sugar) |
| **g** | before a, o, u | hard *g* | {{gato}} `GAH-too`, {{gosto}} `GOHS-too` |
| **g** | before e, i | *zh* (as in *measure*) | {{gente}} `ZHEH~-tchee` (people), {{gelo}} `ZHEH-loo` (ice) |
| **gu**, **qu** | before e, i | *g*, *k* (the **u** is silent) | {{guerra}} `GEH-hah` (war), {{queijo}} `KAY-zhoo`, {{aqui}} `ah-KEE` |
| **qu**, **gu** | before a, o | *kw*, *gw* (the **u** is pronounced) | {{quatro}} `KWAH-troo` (four), {{água}} `AH-gwah` (water) |
| **j** | always | *zh* | {{janeiro}} `zhah-NAY-roo`, {{hoje}} `OH-zhee`, {{jogo}} `ZHOH-goo` |
| **s** | at the start, or **ss** | *s* | {{sol}} `SAW-oo`, {{passo}} `PAH-soo` (step) |
| **s** | between vowels | *z* | {{casa}} `KAH-zah`, {{mesa}} `MEH-zah` (table), {{rosa}} `HAW-zah` |
| **z** | always | *z* | {{zero}} `ZEH-roo`, {{feliz}} `feh-LEES` (final **z** often sounds like *s*) |
| **x** | most words | *sh* | {{xícara}} `SHEE-kah-rah`, {{peixe}} `PAY-shee` (fish) |
| **x** | some words | *ks* | {{táxi}} `TAHK-see`, {{tóxico}} `TAWK-see-koo` |
| **x** | some words | *z* | {{exemplo}} `eh-ZEH~-ploo`, {{exame}} `eh-ZAH-mee` |
| **ch** | always | *sh* | {{chá}} `shah` (tea), {{chuva}} `SHOO-vah` (rain) |

::: dica
**The x has four sounds:** *sh* ({{caixa}}), *ks* ({{táxi}}), *z* ({{exemplo}}), *s* ({{próximo}} `PRAW-see-moo`). There is no rule you can apply from the spelling; learn each new word with its sound. The good news: the *sh* sound is by far the most common.
:::

### Three digraphs you will see in every sentence

| Written | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **lh** | `ly`, like the *lli* in *million* | {{filho}} `FEE-lyoo` (son), {{trabalho}} `trah-BAH-lyoo` (work) |
| **nh** | `ny`, like the *ni* in *onion* | {{amanhã}} `ah-mah-NYAH~` (tomorrow), {{vinho}} `VEE-nyoo` (wine) |
| **ch** | `sh` | {{chave}} `SHAH-vee` (key), {{chocolate}} |

### Two consonants that change at the end of a syllable

**Final L sounds like *oo*.** At the end of a word or before a consonant, the Portuguese **l** is no longer the English *l*. It becomes a short *oo*: {{Brasil}} `brah-ZEE-oo`, {{hotel}} `oh-TEH-oo`, {{total}} `toh-TAH-oo`, {{alto}} `AH-oo-too` (tall), {{sol}} `SAW-oo`. At the start of a syllable, the **l** stays a normal *l*: {{livro}}, {{leite}} `LAY-tchee` (milk).

**T and D before *i* become *tch* and *dj*.** In most of Brazil (São Paulo, Rio, Belo Horizonte, Brasília, Salvador and many other places), the letters **t** and **d** change when they come before an **i** sound. That includes the **-i** you hear in a weak final **-e**:

| Written | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ti | `tchee` | {{tio}} `TCHEE-oo` (uncle), {{partir}} `par-TCHEER` (to leave) |
| di | `djee` | {{dia}} `DJEE-ah` (day), {{cidade}} `see-DAH-djee` (city) |
| -te (final) | `-tchee` | {{noite}} `NOY-tchee`, {{parte}} `PAR-tchee` |
| -de (final) | `-djee` | {{grande}} `GRAH~-djee`, {{verdade}} `ver-DAH-djee` (truth) |

This happens in {{dia}}, in {{tarde}} `TAR-djee` (afternoon) and in {{boa noite}} `BOH-ah NOY-tchee`, three phrases you will say today. In some regions of the Northeast and the South, people still say a plain *t* and *d*, and everyone understands both. Learn the *tch* and *dj* version; it is what most Brazilians say.

## 1.9 The Portuguese R

The letter **r** has several sounds, and English speakers usually make the wrong one everywhere. Forget the English *r* completely (the one with the tongue curled back). Portuguese has two main sounds:

::: passo 1 The "h" R: at the start of a word, after n, l, s, and in rr
This sound is a breath from the back of the throat, close to the English *h* in *hot*. In Rio it is a little rougher, in São Paulo it is softer, in the countryside of São Paulo and Minas it may be a retroflex sound, and all of them are understood.

{{rua}} `HOO-ah` (street), {{rio}} `HEE-oo` (river), {{carro}} `KAH-hoo` (car), {{Israel}} `ees-hah-EH-oo`, {{honra}} `OH~-hah` (honor)
:::

::: passo 2 The tap R: between vowels, and after a consonant inside a word
This is a quick flick of the tongue tip against the ridge behind the upper teeth. English makes the same sound in American *butter*, *ladder* and *better*.

{{caro}} `KAH-roo` (expensive), {{hora}} `OH-rah` (hour), {{para}} `PAH-rah`, {{obrigado}}, {{três}} `trehss`, {{livro}}
:::

At the **end of a word** (**falar**, **comer**, **amor**), the r varies a lot: a soft tap, an *h*, or almost nothing in fast speech. In this book, the guide writes it as a plain `r` and you can use any of the three. Remember the two examples that show the difference: {{caro}} `KAH-roo` (expensive) and {{carro}} `KAH-hoo` (car). One extra *r* changes the sound **and** the meaning.

## 1.10 Stress: which syllable is strong

In English, you must learn the stress of every word. In Portuguese, you can predict it. There are only three steps.

::: passo 1 Look for a written accent mark
If the word has an accent mark (**´**, **^**, or the tilde **~** in some words), that syllable is the stressed one. The mark overrides every rule: {{café}} `kah-FEH`, {{você}} `voh-SEH`, {{último}} `OOL-tchee-moo`, {{médico}} `MEH-djee-koo`, {{avô}} `ah-VOH`.
:::

::: passo 2 With no mark, look at the ending
If the word ends in **-a, -e, -o, -as, -es, -os, -am, -em, -ens**, the stress falls on the **second-to-last** syllable: {{casa}} `KAH-zah`, {{amigo}} `ah-MEE-goo`, {{obrigado}} `oh-bree-GAH-doo`, {{tarde}} `TAR-djee`, {{homem}} `OH~-meh~ee`, {{falam}} `FAH-lah~oo`.

If the word ends in **-i, -is, -u, -us, -l, -r, -z, -im, -um, -ão, -ã**, the stress falls on the **last** syllable: {{falar}} `fah-LAR`, {{Brasil}} `brah-ZEE-oo`, {{feliz}} `feh-LEES`, {{aqui}} `ah-KEE`, {{jardim}} `zhar-DJEE~` (garden), {{irmã}} `eer-MAH~` (sister), {{coração}} `koh-rah-SOW~`.
:::

::: passo 3 Say it with the stress
Say the stressed syllable slightly longer and clearer; the others are lighter, and the unstressed vowels at the end of the word weaken, as you saw in 1.5.
:::

| Ends in | Stress falls on | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -a, -e, -o, -as, -es, -os, -am, -em, -ens | the second-to-last syllable | {{casa}}, {{amigo}}, {{tarde}}, {{homens}} |
| -i, -is, -u, -us, -l, -r, -z, -im, -um, -ão, -ã | the last syllable | {{aqui}}, {{falar}}, {{papel}}, {{feliz}}, {{irmão}} |
| anything, with a written accent | the accented syllable | {{café}}, {{médico}}, {{ônibus}}, {{país}} |

::: atencao
The accent mark can change the meaning. {{país}} `pah-EESS` (country) is not {{pais}} `PIESS` (parents). {{Está}} (is, from *estar*) is not {{esta}} (this, feminine). When in doubt, the written accent tells you how to say the word.
:::

## 1.11 What each mark does

| Mark | Name | What it tells you | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **´** | {{acento agudo}} | stressed syllable; on a, e, o it is also an open vowel (á é í ó ú) | {{café}}, {{avó}}, {{último}} |
| **^** | {{acento circunflexo}} | stressed syllable, closed (or slightly nasal) vowel (â ê ô) | {{você}}, {{avô}}, {{bebê}} |
| **~** | {{til}} | nasal vowel (ã õ) | {{mãe}}, {{pão}}, {{limões}} |
| **&#96;** | {{acento grave}} | only in the contraction **à**, which you will meet in Lesson 5 | {{à}} (to the) |
| **¸** | {{cedilha}} | the letter **c** sounds like *s* before a, o, u (ç) | {{açaí}}, {{coração}} |

## 1.12 The survival phrases

You will use these in your first hour in Brazil. Say each one aloud three times.

| Portuguese | Guide | Meaning |

| {{Oi!}} | `oy` | Hi! (the everyday greeting) |
| {{Olá!}} | `oh-LAH` | Hello! (a little more formal) |

| {{Boa tarde.}} | `BOH-ah TAR-djee` | Good afternoon. |
| {{Boa noite.}} | `BOH-ah NOY-tchee` | Good evening / Good night. |
| {{Tudo bem?}} | `TOO-doo BEH~ee` | How are you? (literally: all well?) |
| {{Tudo bem, e você?}} | `TOO-doo BEH~ee, ee voh-SEH` | Fine, and you? |
| {{Obrigado. / Obrigada.}} | `oh-bree-GAH-doo / oh-bree-GAH-dah` | Thank you. |
| {{De nada.}} | `djee NAH-dah` | You're welcome. |
| {{Por favor.}} | `poor fah-VOR` | Please. |
| {{Com licença.}} | `koh~ lee-SEH~-sah` | Excuse me (to pass or to enter). |

| {{Até logo.}} | `ah-TEH LOH-goo` | See you soon. |

::: passo 1 Use bom dia, boa tarde and boa noite by the clock
{{Bom dia}} until about noon, {{boa tarde}} from noon until it gets dark, {{boa noite}} after that. Notice that **dia** is masculine and takes {{bom}}, while **tarde** and **noite** are feminine and take {{boa}}. Brazilians use {{boa noite}} both to arrive and to leave, unlike English, which splits *good evening* and *good night*.
:::

::: passo 2 Answer "tudo bem?" with "tudo bem"
{{Tudo bem?}} is a greeting, not a medical question. The answer is almost always {{tudo bem}}, followed by {{e você?}}. It is the Brazilian equivalent of *how's it going?*, and nobody expects a long answer.
:::

::: passo 3 Choose obrigado or obrigada by who is speaking
The ending follows **you**, the speaker, not the person you are thanking. A man says {{obrigado}}; a woman says {{obrigada}}. This is the first time you meet gender agreement in Portuguese, and it will keep coming back.
:::

## 1.13 Read a real text

Read the text aloud, slowly, then read it again, with the guide covered. The first time, check every stressed syllable.

> {{Eu sou a Marina. Moro em São Paulo, no Brasil. Hoje é domingo e a minha família vem aqui em casa. A minha mãe faz um bolo de chocolate, o meu pai prepara um café e o meu irmão traz pão. Tudo está pronto e a minha família está muito feliz.}}

Guide: `EH-oo SOH ah mah-REE-nah. MOH-roo eh~ee sow~ POW-loo, noo brah-ZEE-oo. ## 1.16 Pocket summary: Lesson 1

::: resumo
- **Alphabet:** 26 letters; names to remember: **g** = {{gê}}, **j** = {{jota}}, **e** = {{é}}, **r** = {{erre}}, **x** = {{xis}}, **h** = {{agá}} (silent).
- **Pure vowels:** no glide. Final **-e** = weak *ee*, final **-o** = weak *oo*, final **-a** = relaxed *ah*.
- **Open and closed:** ´ marks an open vowel ({{café}}), ^ a closed one ({{você}}).
- **Nasal vowels:** {{ã}}, {{õ}}, **-m**, **-n** before a consonant. Say the vowel through the nose and stop: {{bom}} `BOH~`.
- **Consonants:** **c** before e, i = *s*; **ç** = *s*; **g** before e, i = **j** = *zh*; **gu/qu** before e, i: silent **u**; **lh** = *ly*; **nh** = *ny*; **ch** = **x** = *sh*; **s** between vowels = *z*.
- **R:** initial and **rr** = *h*; between vowels = tap.
- **T, D before *i*:** *tch*, *dj*: {{dia}}, {{noite}}.
- **Final L** = *oo*: {{Brasil}} `brah-ZEE-oo`.
- **Stress:** accent mark wins; otherwise **-a, -e, -o, -s, -m** = second-to-last; **-i, -u, -l, -r, -z, -ão** = last.
- **Politeness:** {{bom dia}} (masc.), {{boa tarde}}, {{boa noite}} (fem.); {{obrigado}} (man), {{obrigada}} (woman).
:::
